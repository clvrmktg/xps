# Understanding the $2,000 Customs Brokerage Threshold for FedEx, UPS, and DHL Shipments in The Bahamas

An international shipment is moving smoothly through an express courier network. Tracking updates show it leaving a Florida hub, arriving in Nassau, and apparently progressing toward delivery.

Then an email arrives from the courier's customs department advising that a customs broker will be required before the shipment can be released.

For many importers, this notification comes as a surprise.

Previous shipments may have been cleared directly through FedEx, UPS, or DHL without any outside involvement, leading businesses to assume the process would remain entirely automatic.

In reality, when a shipment is flagged for independent brokerage, the difference often comes down to a specific threshold: **$2,000.**

## Why Smaller Shipments Often Feel Seamless

One of the advantages of integrated courier networks such as FedEx, UPS, and DHL is that transportation, tracking, customs processing, and final delivery are often handled within the same system.

For lower-value shipments, the process can feel almost invisible to the importer.

The courier collects the shipment, moves it through its international network, prepares the necessary documentation, facilitates clearance, collects applicable duties and taxes, and delivers the goods.

From the customer's perspective, the entire process appears seamless.

This experience often leads importers to assume that all air freight shipments operate the same way regardless of value.

They do not.

## What Changes at $2,000?

In many cases, shipments with a declared value of $2,000 or more require a formal Customs entry rather than the simplified procedures commonly used for lower-value courier shipments.

This means the shipment must be processed as an individual import transaction rather than as part of the courier's standard clearance workflow.

As a result, the courier may advise that a licensed customs broker is required before the shipment can be released.

This often catches businesses off guard because nothing about the physical shipment appears different.

The goods are moving through the same courier network, arriving on the same aircraft, and destined for the same address.

What changes is the Customs process required to release the cargo.

## Why a Customs Broker Becomes Necessary

Many importers assume that if a courier can clear a $1,500 shipment, it should be able to clear a $5,000 shipment in exactly the same way.

The reality is that formal entries involve additional responsibilities.

Depending on the shipment, this may include:

* tariff classification using the correct {{< abbr title="Harmonized System" text="HS" >}} codes;
* importer identification requirements;
* permit verification where applicable;
* supporting commercial documentation;
* Customs representation; and
* formal entry submission through Click2Clear.

Because these requirements carry additional responsibilities and legal obligations, the courier may require a licensed customs broker to prepare and submit the formal entry on behalf of the importer.

The broker becomes responsible for coordinating the declaration, supporting documentation, and cargo release process.

## Delays Can Become Expensive

Once a shipment arrives, time becomes an important factor.

Courier facilities and airport cargo terminals have limited storage capacity, and shipments that remain uncleared for extended periods may become subject to storage charges.

In some cases, couriers may advise that storage fees can begin accumulating after a specified number of days following arrival.

Importers are often surprised to learn that appointing a customs broker does not automatically release the cargo.

The broker must still gather documentation, review the shipment, prepare the entry, and satisfy any permit or Customs requirements before clearance can proceed.

Where invoices, permits, or shipment information are unavailable, delays may occur even after a broker has been appointed.

This is why early preparation is important.

## What Should You Do If You Receive This Notification?

If FedEx, UPS, or DHL advises that a customs broker is required, the best response is to begin gathering documentation immediately.

At a minimum, importers should locate:

* the commercial invoice;
* the air waybill (AWB);
* any applicable import permits;
* business information where applicable; and
* shipment references provided by the carrier.

Providing these documents early helps reduce delays and allows the broker to begin preparing the Customs entry before storage charges or other complications arise.

## The Document Package Your Broker Will Need

A customs broker does not automatically receive every document required to clear a shipment.

To prepare a formal entry, the broker must often combine information received from both the importer and the carrier.

Depending on the shipment, the broker may require:

| Document                        | Purpose                                                                  |
| ------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Commercial Invoice              | Establishes the transaction value and describes the goods being imported |
| Air Waybill (AWB)               | Identifies the shipment and links it to the carrier's records            |
| Manifest Number                 | Used to identify and locate the cargo within the Customs system          |
| Tax Identification Number (TIN) | Required for commercial importers                                        |
| Business Licence                | May be required for business-related imports                             |
| Import Permits                  | Required for regulated or restricted goods                               |
| Supporting Documentation        | Additional information requested by Customs or other government agencies |

Having these documents available before the shipment arrives can significantly improve the clearance process.

## Not All Air Freight Providers Operate This Way

Another common misconception is that all air freight providers offer the same level of service as FedEx, UPS, or DHL.

They do not.

Integrated courier networks are unique because they combine transportation, tracking, clearance support, and delivery within a single operating structure.

Other air freight providers may operate differently.

For example:

### Integrated Couriers (FedEx, UPS, DHL)

Designed for high-speed, standardized shipments with extensive in-house support.

### Traditional Airport-to-Airport Cargo

The airline transports the cargo, but the importer is responsible for arranging clearance and collecting the shipment.

### Freight Forwarders and Consolidators

These providers offer varying service levels. Some may include brokerage support while others require separate arrangements for Customs clearance.

Understanding which type of service is being used can help businesses avoid surprises when cargo arrives.

## Where a Logistics Partner Fits In

FedEx, UPS, and DHL are excellent solutions for many shipments, particularly urgent documents, replacement parts, samples, and lower-value commercial imports.

However, as import activity increases, businesses often require support that extends beyond transportation alone.

This may include:

* customs brokerage;
* permit coordination;
* warehousing;
* inventory management;
* distribution;
* project logistics; and
* ongoing import compliance support.

A shipment may still move internationally through FedEx, UPS, or DHL while a local logistics provider handles brokerage, warehousing, distribution, or other downstream requirements within The Bahamas.

## In Simple Terms

FedEx, UPS, and DHL provide efficient and convenient solutions for many imports into The Bahamas.

However, shipments that require a formal Customs entry may also require the involvement of a licensed customs broker.

Understanding this distinction helps businesses prepare documentation early, avoid unnecessary delays, and ensure cargo moves smoothly through the clearance process.

If you receive a notification advising that a customs broker is required, acting quickly can help prevent storage charges and keep your shipment moving.
